Good brief. Let me take the Cursor analogy apart properly first, because most "Cursor for X" products copy the wrong thing — they copy the chat panel, when the chat panel is the least important part.
Why Cursor actually works
Cursor sits on three things PM work doesn't have:
A substrate. Code lives in one place, in one format, versioned, machine-readable, locally present. The agent doesn't need integrations to know the truth — it reads the repo. Pi is the purest demonstration of this: the model gets just four tools — read, write, edit, and bash — and uses those to fulfill requests, with project instructions loaded at startup from AGENTS.md, and capabilities added on-demand via skills and extensions. The harness can be that thin because the substrate is that rich. npmPi
Cheap verification. Code compiles or it doesn't. Tests pass or they don't. AI output gets checked in seconds by a machine, so hallucination is annoying rather than fatal.
A native unit of change. The diff. There's a whole review culture and tooling built around inspecting a proposed change before it lands.
Why PM work has none of these
No substrate. Product truth is smeared across Jira, Slack, call transcripts, support tickets, Amplitude, Notion, and — mostly — the PM's head. The PM is the integration layer, running swivel-chair synthesis all day, and the synthesis evaporates when they context-switch. This is your seams thesis, and I think it's right — but I'd push it one level deeper: the root cause is that product truth has no HEAD. There is no place to pull the current state of "what we know, what we've decided, what we've promised whom." Every doc is a snapshot that starts rotting the moment the next customer call happens. Status theater, alignment drift, meeting amnesia, answering the same question three different ways to three stakeholders — all downstream symptoms of statelessness.
No compiler. A PRD is "correct" when engineering, design, and leadership agree it reflects reality and intent. Verification is social, therefore expensive. This is precisely why the verification tax exists: ChatGPT drafts a confident PRD in 30 seconds with zero grounding, and checking every claim against scattered sources costs more than the drafting saved. Generic AI doesn't reduce PM work; it converts writing work into auditing work at a bad exchange rate.
No unit of change. Here's the one I'd add to your Value Chain model: the atomic unit of PM work is the decision, and no tool on the market treats decisions as first-class objects. Jira has tickets, Notion has docs, Productboard has features — nobody has decisions. Signals → Insights → Artifacts → Alignment hides the actual work product in the middle: insights don't become artifacts directly, they become artifacts via decisions, and that's the hop that currently lives nowhere.
And one difference about the user, which drives the entire interaction model: PMs won't configure, won't prompt-engineer, won't touch a terminal — but they are professional reviewers. They spend all day approving designs, redlining docs, triaging tickets, making calls. So the interface primitive shouldn't be the prompt. It should be the review.
The core inversion
Today, artifacts are primary: truth gets written into a PRD, then rots. The design move that makes this an OS rather than another destination app is to invert that:
A versioned product state — the Brain — is primary. Artifacts are projections compiled from it. When the state changes, the projections get redlined automatically. The OS mapping is literal: the Brain is the file system, background agents are the daemons, connectors are the drivers, playbooks are the programs, and the review surfaces are the shell.
The Product Brain, precisely
A small, typed, versioned graph. Small on purpose — ontology sprawl is how these products die. Six object types:

Signal — atomic, immutable evidence: a support ticket, a 40-second call clip, a churn anomaly, a competitor release note. Always source-linked and timestamped.
Insight — a synthesized claim ("SMB churns because onboarding requires IT involvement") citing one or more signals, with a confidence level, a freshness date, and an owner. Insights decay: unrefreshed ones get flagged, not silently trusted.
Decision — the keystone object: what was decided, when, by whom, on which evidence, superseding which prior decision. Lifecycle: proposed → made → superseded/expired.
Bet — a problem + hypothesis + success metric, linking decisions and insights. The roadmap stops being a document and becomes a view over bets.
Artifact — PRD, exec update, one-pager. Each factual claim inside it can be bound to a Brain node.
Stakeholder — who cares about what, their stance on open decisions, and what they were last told.

Three invariants: every node carries provenance or is visibly marked as inference; nothing enters the Brain silently — agents propose, the PM ratifies; and nothing is deleted, only superseded — the supersession chain is the answer to "why did we change our mind," which is gold for onboarding and roadmap defenses.
Cold start: the Brain is compiled, not curated. Nobody will populate an ontology by hand. Connect Slack, the meeting recorder, Jira/Linear, and the doc store read-only; background agents extract candidate signals, insights, and decisions into a review queue. The week-one promise has to be: value with zero behavior change — connect read-only on Monday, and by Friday there's an accurate auto-drafted status update and a starter decision log, every line with a receipt. That's the demo moment.
The interaction model — no CLI, no commands
Everything above surfaces through five GUI concepts. The primary verb everywhere is approve/edit/reject, never "prompt."
The Desk. The home screen is not a chat box — a blank chat box is a CLI in a dress. It's an anticipatory brief: two decisions await your call, one insight shifted (with what it affects), the pricing PRD went stale because Thursday's decision superseded section 3 (redline ready), your Friday exec update is drafted. This is the Tab-completion of Cursor translated to a day: the system pre-stages the next action.
The Inbox. Every agent output lands here as a card: one claim or one diff, with receipts, and approve / edit / reject / snooze. Cards are sized to be judged in seconds; the whole queue should clear in under ten minutes a day, with bulk-accept for patterns the PM has ratified before. Accept/reject/edit is also the tuning signal.
Ask. A plain-language question bar over the Brain: "What did we promise Acme about SSO?" → answer, plus chips linking to the call moment and the decision node. The hard rule that kills the verification tax: every generated sentence asserting a fact carries a receipt, and anything the model inferred is visually marked as inference — never silently blended with sourced claims. Verification collapses from re-derivation to spot-checking citations. Discoverability comes from suggested questions, not command syntax.
Living Documents. The artifact editor where claims are bound to nodes. Upstream change → stale badge → proposed redline, accepted with one click. Drafting works by interview, not blank prompt: the agent asks the five questions a good chief of staff would ("who's explicitly out of scope? what's the metric?") and drafts section by section. This also defuses the role-identity anxiety your research surfaced — the product's stance, in its very grammar, is PM as editor-in-chief, AI as staff. It drafts for you; it never speaks as you.
Broadcasts. One decision graph, many projections: exec summary, eng standup note, sales enablement blurb, customer changelog — same facts, audience-shaped tone, plus a ledger of who was told what and when. "What does sales believe vs. what's currently true" becomes an answerable query. Outbound never auto-sends, ever, at any autonomy level.
Autonomy is a per-playbook dial in plain language — Draft only / Propose to my inbox / File automatically — not agent-config jargon.
The pi-shaped architecture underneath
What pi proves is that a deliberately minimal core stays viable when features other tools bake in can instead be built as extensions, skills, or installed packages. The translation: DEV Community
A thin agent runtime with roughly six tools — query_brain, propose_node, draft_artifact, diff_artifact, query_connector, request_review — and everything else lives in userland:
Playbooks are the skills analog: a plain-language procedure + template + autonomy setting ("Every Friday, draft my exec update from this week's decisions and metric moves; flag anything contradicting the Q3 strategy"). Critically, PMs don't write them — they teach them: the system watches how a PM edits its third status-update draft and offers to distill the pattern, then shows the playbook back as editable plain English. Shared playbooks across a team become the org's operating manual — and your switching cost.
The Product Charter is the AGENTS.md analog: strategy, personas, terminology, strong opinions ("we never compete on price") — pinned context loaded into every agent run, editable as a document because PMs already know how to edit documents.
Connectors are the extensions analog. Ship few and deep rather than many and shallow — connector depth is both your biggest cost and your real moat.
Same architecture as pi, opposite shell: pi's users live in a terminal and edit markdown; yours live in queues and documents and never see any of it. The startup logic is the same though — you don't hard-code fifty PM features, you ship a harness plus a Brain and let the playbook library accrete.
The wedge
Don't launch the OS. Pick the object with the highest value per review-minute. Signals are high-volume, low unit value, and that seam is crowded — Enterpret and Cycle already own VoC→insight. Decisions are low-volume, high unit value, completely unowned, and everything else attaches to them.
So: (1) meetings + Slack → a decision log with receipts; (2) status updates auto-drafted from that log — kills status theater, delivers a visible win weekly; (3) bindings and living PRDs; (4) broadcasts and the stakeholder ledger. Each stage earns the trust and permissions for the next connector. Against the landscape: feedback tools own one seam, enterprise search (Glean, Rovo) answers questions but holds no state and runs no workflow, and doc-AI leaves artifacts primary and rotting. The gap is exactly a stateful brain plus review-native workflow.
Things worth pressure-testing in the next round of interviews: whether "decision" as the atomic object matches PMs' mental model or fights it; whether receipts actually earn trust (watch if they click through, and when they stop); whether they'll clear a daily inbox — the Superhuman question; and who owns the Brain when five PMs share one product area, because governance is a first-class design surface here, not an edge case.
